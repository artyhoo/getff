## What this umbrella proved (the load-bearing claims)

- **Depth is a named product decision, not a flag combination.** S1's inventory enumerates the payload per profile and shows no comfort/guard hook is lost between depths — the claim is measured against the inventory on every lane, not against one demo run (T-BDU-A counter).
- **A vendor handshake is only proven by a completion that the vendor billed.** S4's step D is the whole lesson: the single-call form echoed a matching profile id and asserted nothing. Provisioning flows that stop at «aif found the key» are `#contract-that-cannot-fail`.
- **A `files` allowlist is a testable artifact.** R1's tarball cell paired RED→GREEN per entry, 10 of 14 arms RED-provable, the other 4 marked `UNVALIDATED-by-this-cell` with the reason — kept rather than dropped, because dropping them would silently break three bins when their deferred runtime-dep gap closes.
- **The `#contract-that-cannot-fail` shape is the dominant defect class here, and only running the arm finds it.** Across S2, S4 and R1 the same shape recurred: a block present, commands executing, exit 0, and the defect class ungated — a `length > 100` size check printing «loaded», a step labelled «assert a rule fires» that planted nothing, a fork discriminator returning rc=0 under both options, a jq syntax error swallowed by its own fallback. Reading never caught one of them.
- **The container→host boundary is where acceptance actually happens.** Every stage that ran in aif was accepted only after its declared contract passed on the **host** — R1's `host-verify.sh` returned 2/2 PASS on Darwin, and Channel A egress kept `.husky/pre-push` in the loop, which immediately caught two MD040 violations a container-side husky bypass had left in the freeze record.
